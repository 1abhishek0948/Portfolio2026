NRB Dollar Card Limits and Rules Explained for Nepal Residents

Plain-language explainer of the NRB rules and limits that apply to dollar cards and foreign-currency accounts for Nepal residents, and what to check before you spend.

Abhishek Thakur Published Oct 27, 2026 · Updated Oct 27, 2026· 4 min read

The single most useful thing you can do before buying a dollar card is understand the rules the National Bank of Nepal (NRB) puts around foreign currency for residents. Most dollar-card problems in Nepal are not card problems at all. They are "the limit changed and nobody told me" problems. This page is a plain-language checklist of the rules that matter, and where to confirm the exact, current numbers.

A strong warning up front: regulatory limits are not the stable facts like "a card needs a card." They are the things that get revised, and when they do, your old understanding is wrong. This page teaches you what to check and why, not the number to trust. For the current figure, ask your bank's foreign-currency desk or the NRB directly. This is general information, not legal or financial advice.

What the NRB actually regulates

The NRB sits on top of every bank's card and foreign-currency rules. Three areas drive your dollar-card experience:

  1. How much foreign currency a resident can hold in an account or on a card.
  2. How much a resident can spend through that foreign currency over a period.
  3. How the foreign currency must have entered Nepal to be accepted at all.

Your individual bank then implements those rules, sometimes more strictly, and adds its own limits on top. So the real ceiling on your card is the NRB limit and your bank's limit, whichever is lower.

The three numbers to confirm, not assume

Instead of memorizing figures, commit to checking three specific numbers with your bank. They are the ones that change and that bite you.

Check What to ask Why it matters
Holding ceiling "What is the current NRB limit on how much foreign currency I can hold?" Sets the account/card cap
Spend ceiling "What is the limit on how much I can spend with it, and over what period?" Sets your usable budget
Source rule "What proof of source do you need for the foreign currency?" The usual reason a card gets declined

You will hear the current numbers from the desk that is legally bound to be up to date. Write them down, dated. That dated note is your record, because the answer will differ next year.

Why "how it got here" is the real gate

The NRB does not just cap the amount; it conditions on the foreign currency having a legitimate origin. For residents, the clean origins are the ones you can document: foreign-earning employment, freelance or export income, remittance from abroad, or an approved inflow. A card that is fed from a clean, documented source moves smoothly. A card whose funding source is fuzzy is where applications stall.

If you are a freelancer, this is your superpower: a platform payout, a client contract, and the credit entry together are exactly the documentation the rules want. See how to get a dollar card in Nepal for the document set.

Rules that shape the card you can get

Because the NRB regulates the underlying account, the card types you can get are not free choices:

  • A USD account only exists in forms the NRB permits residents to hold, and it is linked to a permitted source.
  • A prepaid/loaded foreign-currency card is usually capped at the loaded balance and the NRB ceiling, whichever is lower.
  • A plain international card on a local account avoids most of the foreign-currency paperwork, which is why it is the easy path for small occasional spend.

The dollar card charges guide covers what each of these costs on top of the rules.

What to do if you hit the ceiling

Hitting the NRB ceiling is not a bug to fix; it is a planning input. The healthy responses are:

  • Plan under the limit. Schedule the big payments so they land inside the window you have.
  • Match the method to the ceiling. If your spend is close to or over the limit, a dollar card is the wrong tool for that portion, and you need an alternative method.
  • Re-check the ceiling. The number may have moved since you last looked, and the move may be in your favor.
  • Talk to the desk, not to forums. The bank desk knows the current operational position better than any cached page.

A 30-second pre-spend habit

Before any big foreign charge, run this three-step check:

  1. Is the current NRB ceiling confirmed and recent?
  2. Does my bank's own limit clear the charge amount?
  3. Is my funding source clean and documented for this card?

If all three are yes, the charge should clear. If one is no, that is the reason, and it is the fix. Most "my card was declined" stories in Nepal trace back to one of these three being quietly wrong.

Bottom line: the NRB sets the framework and the ceilings, your bank implements it, and both change. Do not memorize the numbers. Build the habit of confirming the holding ceiling, the spend ceiling, and the source rule with a live source before you spend. That habit is worth more than any dollar card you buy.

FAQ

Does the NRB set one fixed dollar limit for everyone?

The NRB sets the regulatory framework and ceilings for how much foreign currency residents may hold and spend, and those ceilings are updated from time to time. The exact, current figure is what you must confirm with your bank or NRB, because it is the number that changes.

Do the rules treat freelancers differently from salaried people?

The rules treat you as a resident and they attach conditions around how foreign currency entered Nepal. The practical difference is in documentation: a documented source of foreign income makes using the limits straightforward.

What happens if I exceed the limit?

You simply cannot spend or hold beyond the ceiling under the current rules. The realistic fix is to plan payments under the limit, move money within permitted windows, or use an alternative method that fits the ceiling.

Sources and how to verify

Foreign-currency ceilings, card terms and bank fees are set by the NRB and each bank and change; confirm the current limit and fee with your bank before you rely on a number. General information, not financial advice.
