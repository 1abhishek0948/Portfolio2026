Dollar Card Charges in Nepal: Annual Fees, Loading Costs & Hidden Limits

The real costs of a dollar card in Nepal: annual fees, foreign-currency loading charges, conversion spreads, transaction limits and the small charges people forget.

Abhishek Thakur Published Oct 27, 2026 · Updated Oct 27, 2026· 5 min read

Getting the card is half the story. The other half is knowing what it actually costs to own and use it, because the charge structure on a dollar card in Nepal is where people are surprised. This guide breaks the fees into the buckets you will actually face: what the card costs to hold, what it costs to load and move foreign currency, what international settlement adds, and the small charges nobody warns you about.

As always, fees change and differ between banks. Use this as a checklist of the categories to ask about at the branch, then confirm the exact numbers in your bank's published fee schedule for the year you are opening the card.

The fee buckets to expect

Bucket What it is Who pays it
Card issuance One-time cost to produce the card Applicant
Annual / maintenance Recurring fee for holding the card Cardholder
Foreign-currency loading Cost to move USD into the account or onto a prepaid card Account holder
Transaction fee Per transaction or per withdrawal Cardholder
Currency conversion spread Difference between the bank's rate and the mid-market rate Anyone settling a cross-border charge
Over-limit / late Fees if you exceed a limit or pay a card late Cardholder
Card replacement Lost/stolen card reissue Cardholder

The two buckets that quietly kill a dollar card's value are the loading charge and the conversion spread, because both hit every single time you move money internationally.

Annual and maintenance charges

Some banks waive the annual fee if your account stays above a threshold balance, or on premium tiers, and charge it otherwise. This "minimum balance or you pay" pattern is extremely common, so it changes the real cost depending on how much you keep in the account.

Before you open one, ask: is the annual fee fixed, is it waived at a minimum balance, and what happens in the month the fee is billed if your balance dips. A card that is "free" only when you hold a balance you cannot keep becomes an annual charge in practice.

Loading and moving foreign currency

This is the heart of a dollar card's cost. If your card is backed by a USD account, you pay something to get dollars into that account. That cost is sometimes a flat per-transfer charge, sometimes a percentage of the amount, and sometimes folded into the exchange rate itself.

Three things to nail down:

  • Is loading free only within NRB-permitted limits? Many banks load foreign currency up to a ceiling at one rate and charge more above it.
  • What is the spread on loading? The rate the bank buys dollars at is usually not the mid-market rate; that gap is a real cost.
  • Does the source of the dollars matter for pricing? Documented freelance/export earnings are usually treated more smoothly than ad-hoc sources.

If you are loading small, frequent amounts (typical for a freelancer settling a project), the per-transaction component of loading charges adds up faster than the headline percentage suggests.

The conversion spread you cannot see

When a charge settles internationally and the bank converts, it uses its own rate. That rate is set to earn the bank money, so it is slightly worse than the open market. For a one-off hosting payment the difference is small; across many international charges a year, it is a steady leak.

You cannot remove the spread, but you can reduce how often you pay it:

  • Batch payments where possible so you are not converting many tiny amounts.
  • Pay from an account already holding the currency (a USD account charged in USD) instead of converting rupees repeatedly.
  • Confirm whether your card's international settlement is on a card network or via a gateway, since the paths and spreads differ.

Limits are a kind of cost too

Foreign-currency accounts and cards carry ceilings on how much you can hold and spend, set by NRB rules and by each bank. Hitting those ceilings is not a fee, but it behaves like one because it forces you to switch methods, wait, or move funds in ways that add cost and delay.

Read the NRB dollar card rules explainer for the ceilings that apply to residents. If you routinely spend near the limit, that is a signal to plan your payments or pick a method that fits the ceiling.

The small charges people forget

  • SMS / alert fees on some tiers.
  • ATM and withdrawal fees for foreign-currency cards used offline.
  • Card replacement fees if you lose it.
  • Late or over-limit fees on credit-style cards.
  • Dormancy fees if the card sits unused.

None of these is large on their own, but on a card you use occasionally, one or two of them can exceed the annual fee of a "free" card you expected.

How to compare two dollar cards

Do not compare on the annual fee alone. Put both on the same checklist and total a realistic year:

  1. Annual / maintenance fee (and the balance that waives it).
  2. Loading charge structure for your typical transfer size.
  3. Conversion spread on international settlement.
  4. Transaction and withdrawal fees.
  5. The ceiling you would actually hit.
  6. Replacement and dormant fees.

For how to choose the bank, read the best bank for a dollar card in Nepal, and if your goal is simply paying for hosting or subscriptions, see how to pay for foreign hosting from Nepal.

Bottom line: the cost of a dollar card is not one number. It is annual fees plus loading charges plus a conversion spread plus the little per-transaction and dormant fees, all bounded by NRB limits. Add them up for a realistic year, compare two banks on that total, and you will stop being surprised.

FAQ

Are dollar cards free in Nepal?

No. Even when a card is free to issue, you typically pay for foreign-currency loading, card maintenance, or conversion when the charge settles internationally. Read the fee schedule before you decide a card is 'free'.

What is a loading charge?

A loading charge is the cost to move foreign currency into a dollar account (or onto a prepaid dollar card). Banks price this per transaction or as a percentage, and it is one of the most-quoted costs on any foreign-currency product.

Does the exchange-rate spread cost me anything?

Yes. When a local card settles an international charge, the bank converts at its rate, which is usually worse than the mid-market rate. That spread is an effective fee on every cross-border payment.

Sources and how to verify

Foreign-currency ceilings, card terms and bank fees are set by the NRB and each bank and change; confirm the current limit and fee with your bank before you rely on a number. General information, not financial advice.
