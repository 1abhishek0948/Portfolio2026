Receiving Remittance in Nepal: Methods and Fees

How money reaches Nepal as remittance: the main methods, who sends it, the fees people actually pay, and the records that keep the arrival clean.

Abhishek Thakur Published Oct 27, 2026 · Updated Oct 27, 2026· 2 min read

Remittance is one of the clearest forms of foreign money entering Nepal, and most of it moves from Nepali families living abroad to accounts at home. This guide is about the receiving side: the methods, the fees, and the small record that keeps an arrival clean.

Fees, supported channels and limits change by provider and by rule, so confirm the current ones on the sender's side. This is general information, not financial advice.

The main channels

The picture is a small number of lanes:

Channel What to check
Bank wire Speed, intermediary fees, and the documents the receiving bank may want
Licensed remittance operator The sender's fee, and where it lands
Digital remittance service Support, the receiving method, and the cost to the sender

The pattern that matters to the receiver: the sender picks the lane, and the receiver's job is to make sure it lands in the right account and is dated and documented on arrival.

The two costs to keep straight

One is the transfer fee, which is set by the channel and usually paid by the sender, but confirm who actually pays for the specific transfer. The other is quieter: the cost of a mismatched arrival, where the money lands somewhere that is hard to move from, or arrives without the note that explains it. That second cost is the one people do not plan for, and it is why the receiving side needs a habit, not just the sender needing a channel.

The record that keeps it clean

On the receiver's side, one dated line per arrival:

  • The date and the amount.
  • Who sent it, and through which channel.
  • Which account it landed in.

That line is what turns a large or repeated arrival from a question into a fact. Keep it, and the money is easy to explain; drop it, and every arrival is a small thing to reconstruct.

The connection to the rules

Because remittance is foreign money coming in, it meets the same "documented source" logic as any other foreign-currency flow. See NRB foreign currency rules for the shape of that, and keep the arrival record as the proof. If you are the one sending out, the mirror image is sending money abroad from Nepal.

A sensible default

Confirm the channel and who pays the fee on the sender's side, make the money land in a normal account, and write the one dated line for the arrival. Three small moves, and the remittance is clean on the side that actually holds it.

Bottom line: receiving remittance is a documented arrival, not a mystery. Confirm the lane and the fee with the sender, land it in the right account, and keep one dated line per arrival, and the money stays easy to explain every time.

FAQ

What are the main ways remittance reaches Nepal?

Mostly through bank channels and licensed money-remittance operators, and increasingly through digital services. The sender's choice of channel sets the fee and the speed, and the receiver's job is to make sure the arrival is documented and lands in the right account.

Does the receiver pay a fee?

Usually the sender pays the transfer fee, but that depends on the channel and who initiated it. Confirm on the sender's side, because the practical answer is set by the service being used, not by a single rule.

What record should the receiver keep?

The arrival: the date, the amount, who sent it and through which channel, and the account it landed in. That one dated line is what keeps a large or repeated arrival easy to explain later.

Sources and how to verify

Foreign-currency ceilings, card terms and bank fees are set by the NRB and each bank and change; confirm the current limit and fee with your bank before you rely on a number. General information, not financial advice.
