How to Get a Dollar Card in Nepal: Bank Guide, Fees & Requirements (2026)

Step-by-step guide to getting a dollar card in Nepal: which banks offer it, the documents you need, NRB rules, activation and how to use it for foreign payments.

Abhishek Thakur Published Oct 27, 2026 · Updated Oct 27, 2026· 5 min read

A dollar card is one of the most common money questions from Nepali developers, freelancers and small businesses. You need it to pay for things that only accept international cards: foreign web hosting, domain renewals, SaaS subscriptions, advertising platforms, and some payment gateways. This guide walks through what a dollar card actually is in Nepal, who can get one, the documents banks usually ask for, and how to avoid the mistakes that make the process slow.

Before you start, a note: card products, fees and foreign-currency rules are set by the National Bank of Nepal (NRB) and each commercial bank, and they change. Treat the names, amounts and timeframes below as a 2026 starting point, and always confirm the current terms with your own bank branch before you rely on them. This is general information, not financial advice.

What a "dollar card" actually means

The phrase "dollar card" is used three slightly different ways in Nepal, and the difference matters a lot.

  1. A USD-denominated card linked to a foreign-currency account, billed and settled in US dollars.
  2. An international card (Visa/Mastercard) issued on a local account that can be used abroad and for online international charges, with the bank converting at its own rate.
  3. A prepaid/loaded foreign-currency card you top up with dollars, capped at the loaded balance.

Most of the time, when people ask "how to get a dollar card in Nepal", they mean option 1 or option 3: a card that lets you pay foreign vendors without fighting the domestic-rupee ceiling. Option 2 is simply a normal international card and the easiest to get, but it only works for payments the local card actually supports.

Who can apply

Eligibility depends on the bank and the account type, but the pattern is consistent.

  • Residents with a Nepali bank account can usually open a foreign-currency account if they can show how the foreign currency arrived in Nepal legally (this is the core requirement).
  • Freelancers and exporters are the strongest use case, because they have a real flow of foreign earnings that can enter a dollar account.
  • Students and salaried staff may get an international card more easily than a true USD card, because a USD account generally needs a documented source of foreign currency.

If you have genuine foreign income, banks are far more willing to help. The harder part is not the card; it is proving the source of the dollars.

Documents banks typically ask for

Expect a common set; the exact list varies by bank and by whether you are opening a fresh account or linking a card to an existing one.

Document Why it is asked
Citizenship / identity proof Core KYC for any account
Passport-size photos and address proof KYC record
Source-of-income proof To justify a foreign-currency account
Freelance/contract or client invoices Shows the dollars have a legal origin
Existing account statements Links the card to a real account
Letter of intent / application form The bank's own paperwork

If you are a freelancer, your strongest single document is a clean paper trail of foreign earnings: platform payout statements, client contracts, and the bank credit entry that shows the foreign currency arriving. The cleaner that trail, the faster the application moves.

Step-by-step process

  1. Pick your card type. Decide whether you need a true USD card or just an international card that settles in rupees. Many people only need the latter.
  2. Choose the bank. Compare on three things: card type, foreign-currency handling, and monthly/service charges. See the dollar card charges guide before you commit.
  3. Open or link the account. If you need a USD account, complete that first; the card is attached to it.
  4. Apply for the card with your document set.
  5. Activate and set limits. Confirm your transaction limits, whether you can do online/international transactions, and how currency conversion is charged.
  6. Test with a small charge before you rely on it for a large hosting or ad bill.

NRB rules that affect you

The National Bank of Nepal regulates how foreign currency can be held and spent by residents. Two points matter in practice.

  • There are limits on how much foreign currency you can hold and spend as a resident, and they have changed over time. Ask your bank for the current NRB-permitted ceiling before you plan a big spend.
  • How the dollars got into Nepal matters. A foreign-currency account is accepted when the funds have a legitimate, documented origin. Earnings from genuine freelance or export work fit; vague sources do not.

For the rules and limits, read the NRB dollar card rules explainer. The numbers move, so treat the page as a checklist, not a statute.

Common mistakes that slow you down

  • Asking a branch for a "dollar card" without saying which type you need, then waiting on the wrong product.
  • Applying without a documented source of foreign currency, which is the single most common reason a USD account gets rejected.
  • Forgetting to enable online and international transactions; a card that is blocked for e-commerce does nothing for hosting or ads.
  • Assuming the rupee conversion is "free". International settlement carries a conversion cost.
  • Ignoring monthly service charges on a card you only use a few times a year.

FAQ

Does everyone in Nepal get a dollar card?

Not automatically. A dollar card is usually a card that pays out in a foreign currency or is linked to a foreign-currency (dollar) account. Some banks issue a USD card for accounts denominated in foreign currency; others let you make a local card bill internationally. Ask your bank whether it offers a dollar-denominated card or an internationally settled card.

Do I need a dollar account to get a dollar card?

In most cases yes. The card is attached to a foreign-currency account, so the bank will want you to open or link a USD account first, and show how the dollars entered the account. Confirm the exact account type your bank supports.

How long does a dollar card take to arrive?

Issuance commonly takes a few days to a couple of weeks once documents and the account are in place. Delivery and activation times vary by bank and delivery method, so ask your branch for a realistic timeframe.

Sources and how to verify

Foreign-currency ceilings, card terms and bank fees are set by the NRB and each bank and change; confirm the current limit and fee with your bank before you rely on a number. General information, not financial advice.
