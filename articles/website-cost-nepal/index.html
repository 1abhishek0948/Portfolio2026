How Much Does a Website Cost in Nepal?

By Abhishek ThakurSep 9, 202610 min read

The honest answer is "it depends on scope" — but that answer helps nobody until it's explained. Here is how website pricing in Nepal actually works, what drives it, and how to make sure you pay for value instead of jargon.

Why quotes vary so much

Ask five providers for "a website for my business" and you can get quotes that differ by five times or more. That's not (only) overcharging — it's that "a website" means different things to different people. One quote covers a template with your text pasted in; another covers custom design, copywriting, photography, search-engine setup and months of support. Comparing those two numbers is like comparing a bicycle and a bus because both have wheels.

The realistic tiers

With the caveat that these are typical Nepali market ranges, not promises, here's how the tiers usually look:

  • DIY or free options (NPR 0 to a few thousand). Website builders and free templates. Real cost: your time, and the limits of what a template can do. Fine for testing an idea; weak for a serious business presence.
  • A simple business website (roughly NPR 15,000–50,000). A handful of well-built pages, mobile-friendly, with contact details, proper titles and descriptions, and working forms. What most Nepali small businesses actually need.
  • A custom-designed or feature-rich website (roughly NPR 50,000–150,000+). Bespoke design, catalogue or booking features, multiple languages, more pages and content work.
  • A web application / platform / SaaS (NPR 150,000 into the lakhs, or ongoing development). Custom software: user accounts, dashboards, APIs, databases, payments. Budgeted like the software project it is, because that's what it is.

Where a quote falls inside (or outside) these ranges depends on the factors below — and on who's quoting.

What actually drives the cost

  • Pages and content. Five pages with your text cost less than thirty pages someone has to structure and write.
  • Design: template vs custom. A template is cheaper and faster; custom design costs more and, done well, fits your business instead of fitting your business into it.
  • Features. Contact form vs booking system vs user accounts vs payments — each step up is genuinely more engineering.
  • Content and photography. Someone has to write and photograph. If you provide these, you save real money.
  • Quality of the technical work. Speed, mobile correctness, search-engine basics, accessibility — invisible when done, expensive to fix when not.
  • Support after launch. Included by some, charged separately by others, missing entirely from the cheapest quotes.

The costs people forget

Two website bills arrive after the first one: recurring costs — hosting, domain renewal, and any maintenance arrangement — and change costs, when prices or services change and the site must follow. Ask about both before committing. A .com.np domain, by the way, is free to register through the official portal with your citizenship or business documents; the paid part is hosting. For a local business, recurring costs should be modest and predictable — if they're presented as large and vague, ask why.

How to pay for value, not theatre

  • Agree on scope in writing: which pages, which features, what content, what "done" means.
  • See live work on your own phone before choosing anyone.
  • Confirm you own the domain, hosting, and code — in your name, from day one.
  • Be suspicious of guaranteed rankings and of "we'll throw in 20 extra pages". You don't need 20 pages; you need the right five.
  • Start small and honest: a simple site done well, extended as your business proves the need, beats an expensive platform you half-use.
