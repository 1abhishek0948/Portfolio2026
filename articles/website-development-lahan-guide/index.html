Website Development in Lahan: A Practical Guide for Local Businesses

By Abhishek ThakurSep 9, 202612 min read

More people in Lahan search for your business on their phone than ask a neighbour. This guide, written by a developer who grew up in Lahan, explains what a local business actually needs from a website — and how to avoid paying for what it doesn't.

Why a website matters in a town like Lahan

Lahan has grown into one of the busiest commercial towns of Siraha district and eastern Madhesh Province. When someone here needs a shop, a tuition centre, a clinic or a service business, the first instinct is increasingly the same as anywhere: they search, they check what they find, and they call whoever looks real. A business with no website — or with a broken one — is invisible at exactly that moment.

A Facebook page helps, but it isn't a website. Social platforms decide how your information is displayed, whether customers can find you in a search, and what happens to your content. Your own website is the one place where your business's name, services, location and contact details are presented your way — permanently.

First question: what kind of website do you need?

Most local businesses need one of four things, and knowing which one you need is the biggest money-saver in this whole guide:

  • A simple presence website. Three to six pages: who you are, what you offer, where you are, how to contact you. This is what most shops, clinics, and services in Lahan actually need.
  • A catalogue or showcase website. Your products or work displayed properly, with prices or enquiries — common for furniture, tailoring, electronics, photography, and construction-related businesses.
  • A booking or enquiry-driven site. For tuition centres, clinics and salons: forms that turn visitors into appointments.
  • A full platform or application. Ordering, membership, payments, dashboards. Real software — worth it only when a simpler site can't do the job.

The mistake I see most often is paying for level four when the business needs level one. A good developer's first job is telling you which level you're actually at.

What a good local business website includes

  • Working properly on a phone. Most of your customers will visit on mobile data, often on budget phones. The site must load fast and be easy to use with a thumb, not just look good on a designer's laptop.
  • Your real information, everywhere. Name, address, phone, hours, location — consistent across the site and any online directory or map listing. Consistency is what makes search engines (and customers) trust the details.
  • Clear pages for what you actually do. One honest page about each service beats five vague pages stuffed with the same phrases.
  • Click-to-call and WhatsApp links. In Nepal, the phone call is still king. Make it one tap.
  • Basic search-engine basics. A proper page title, a description, a sitemap — the invisible plumbing that lets Google and other search engines index your site properly.
  • Fast images. Photos of Lahan businesses are often shot on phones and uploaded at full size. Compressed, modern-format images make the site dramatically faster on mobile data.

About the .com.np domain

A detail many Nepali businesses don't know: registering a .com.np domain through the official register.com.np portal is free of charge — you'll need your citizenship or business registration documents, and the domain must match your name or your registered business name. A domain like yourbusiness.com.np signals a local, established business. Hosting is a separate, ongoing cost; reliable options exist from a few thousand rupees a year. Whoever builds your site should register the domain in your name, with your documents — not theirs.

How to choose a web developer in Lahan (or anywhere in Nepal)

Questions worth asking

  • "Show me websites you've built that are live — I want to open them on my phone."
  • "Will the domain, hosting, and code be registered in my name? Who owns what when we're done?"
  • "What happens after launch? Do you fix problems, and how?"
  • "What do you need from me — photos, text, prices — and by when?"

Warning signs

  • No portfolio, or a portfolio that only opens on their laptop
  • You don't get ownership of your own domain or files
  • Promise of "guaranteed first page on Google" — no honest developer can guarantee rankings
  • No discussion of your content or your customers, only of design and price

And one practical note: local doesn't mean worse, and remote doesn't mean better. A developer in Lahan who meets you and understands your customers can beat a distant agency that never asks about them. Judge the work and the answers, not the address.

What it costs, roughly

I've written a full separate article on how much a website costs in Nepal, but the short version: a simple, well-built business website is an affordable, one-time investment; a custom web application is a bigger project with an appropriately bigger budget. Beware of both extremes — suspiciously cheap quotes that cut corners you can't see, and quotes inflated with jargon you can't verify.

After the website: keeping it alive

A website is not a one-time purchase like a signboard. Content changes, prices change, and software needs occasional updates. Agree with your developer, in advance, on how updates will happen and what support costs — a modest maintenance arrangement with someone dependable beats an abandoned "modern" site every time. My own support and maintenance service exists precisely because this is where most local websites fail.
