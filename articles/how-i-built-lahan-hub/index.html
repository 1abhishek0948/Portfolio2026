How I Built LAHAN Hub

Scope, architecture and lessons from building a 21-application Django digital city ecosystem with PostgreSQL and Docker — my largest full-stack project to date.

Abhishek Thakur Published Aug 18, 2026 · Updated Sep 9, 2026 · min read
Email

Why a digital city, not a website

LAHAN Hub started with a framing question: is Lahan better served by ten disconnected websites, or by one platform that treats local life as connected experiences? I chose the second. The platform spans 21 Django applications — a marketplace, a jobs board, community spaces, weather, notifications, everyday utilities and local information — and all of them share one authentication system and one coherent data layer. A person who creates an account to browse the marketplace can post a job, save a listing and get notified without ever feeling the seams.

In practice that meant designing the whole thing up front as a city, then shrinking each application until it did one job well. It is the project where I learned the most about scale — not scale of traffic, but scale of structure.

Designing 21 applications around boundaries

One Django project can hold many applications, and the hardest decision was not what each application should contain, but what it should not contain. I grouped the products around things people do rather than things code does:

  • Commerce and work — the marketplace and the jobs board, the two areas where people transact.
  • Community — spaces where locals, newcomers and businesses introduce themselves to each other.
  • Everyday utilities — weather, local information and the small features that make the platform worth opening on a Tuesday, not just once.
  • Connective tissue — notifications, profiles and settings that the other applications call into.

Defining those boundaries early mattered more than any individual feature. Every time two applications reached past their own data, the fix was rarely more integration — it was deciding which app owned the object and letting the other one ask.

One database, used carefully

The entire ecosystem runs on PostgreSQL, with relationships shared carefully across application boundaries. That care shows up in three habits:

  • Each data model belongs to the application that creates it; other applications read or reference it through predictable structures rather than reaching into columns.
  • Cross-application queries are deliberate. If a marketplace view needs the user's profile, that need is expressed in one clear direction.
  • Migrations are treated as part of the product. With 21 applications evolving, keeping models understandable is what made every other change cheap.

Keeping data models understandable saved me again and again. The moment a schema is ambiguous, every feature built on it inherits the ambiguity.

Docker and the everyday realities

Development and deployment are both containerized with Docker. The point was never spectacular infrastructure — it was repetition with zero drift. The local environment and the live environment share the same shape, configuration lives in environment variables rather than code, and PostgreSQL runs as a service alongside the application. When something works on my machine, "my machine" is a reproducible definition rather than a memory.

For a project of this size, that discipline mattered most in the weeks when I was touching several applications at once. Consistency lets you change five things and worry about only the five things.

What went wrong before it went right

The hardest scale to prepare for was not scale of traffic — it was scale of structure.

Early on, I built features first and placed them later, and the seam showed: similar screens behaved differently because different applications had grown their own conventions. The fix was not a rewrite — it was settling shared conventions for navigation, permissions and notifications, then walking the platform until each application honored them.

The second lesson was about navigation. If navigation mirrors the underlying code structure, users feel engineering; when it mirrors what people need to do, they just use the site. Designing around tasks kept LAHAN Hub usable as it grew.

A fuller summary of the build now lives on the LAHAN Hub project page. If you are planning a local digital platform of your own, write to me through my services or get in touch — building small cities of software is exactly the kind of problem I enjoy.
