Choosing a website development company in Nepal is easy to get wrong, because a strong pitch deck and a sharp price can mask a weak process. The difference between a site you will use for years and one you quietly abandon is rarely the price; it is whether the company does the unglamorous work: clear scope, ownership of the domain and hosting, a maintenance plan, and honesty about what will break. This guide is a set of questions and signals that separate the two.

Every business is different, so treat this as a framework and confirm specifics with each company you are actually considering. This is a buyer's guide, not a recommendation of any firm.

## What "good" actually means for a website

Before you compare companies, know what you are buying, because most scope fights come from not agreeing on this. A good website for you is:

- **Correctly scoped.** It does the specific job you need it to, nothing that does not help.
- **Yours.** You own the domain, the hosting, and the code, and you are not locked into one vendor to make a change.
- **Maintainable.** There is a named process and a cost for fixing it after launch, not a cliff.

Companies that skip one of these are the ones who produce the "it looked great for two months" website.

## The questions that reveal capability

Ask every shortlisted company the same questions and read the answers, not the enthusiasm.

| Question | What a strong answer looks like |
|---|---|
| Show me a live site you still maintain | They can, and they can say what they changed after launch |
| Who owns the domain and the hosting? | You do, or a clear path to transfer to you does |
| What happens after we launch? | A named maintenance scope and a cost, not "we are here" |
| What does 'done' mean here? | A written list of deliverables and acceptance criteria |
| How will I make changes? | A process, with rough cost and time, not "just ask" |
| What breaks first on a project like mine? | A specific risk, which shows they have done it before |

The last one is the sharpest test. A company that names the risk first has built things like yours. A company that lists features instead has not.

## How to read a proposal

A proposal is a forecast, and the useful ones are the honest, boring ones. Check for:

- **Scope in writing**, with the deliverables named, not adjectives.
- **Timeline with stages**, not one deadline.
- **Ownership terms** for the domain, hosting and code.
- **Maintenance terms** for at least the first year.
- **What is out of scope**, which is the most important line and the one vague proposals omit.

If a proposal is all features and no terms, you are buying a pitch, not a plan. Ask for the terms, and treat the answer as the real proposal.

## Red flags that save money

- **No live work to show**, only mockups.
- **"You own it" is not stated**, and the domain or hosting is held by the vendor.
- **No maintenance plan**, or maintenance is a big surprise at the end.
- **One flat price with no scope**, which usually means the scope will be fought over.
- **Resistance to written acceptance criteria**, which is how "done" gets defined.
- **No one named as your point of contact** after sales.

None of these is proof of fraud; each is a reason to slow down and get it in writing.

## Freelancer vs company, and when to hire one or the other

The "company" question is really two: a firm, or a single experienced developer. A firm offers a team and a support desk; a strong individual offers speed, price and a direct line. The right choice depends on scope:

- A focused marketing site: a strong individual can be excellent.
- A larger build with ongoing needs: a firm with maintenance may fit better.

## A shortlist process that works

1. **Write your one-page spec**: the job, the audience, "done" in your words, and your budget band.
2. **Shortlist three** companies or developers who show live, maintained work.
3. **Ask the same seven questions** and read the answers.
4. **Request written terms** for ownership, timeline and maintenance.
5. **Pick the one whose terms you can trust**, not the one with the most features.

The spec you write in step one is worth more than any vendor you pick, because it is what you will judge them against.

**Bottom line:** you are not choosing the company with the best deck; you are choosing the one whose written terms you can trust. Ask the seven questions, insist on ownership and maintenance in writing, and the website you get will be one you can live with, change, and keep.
