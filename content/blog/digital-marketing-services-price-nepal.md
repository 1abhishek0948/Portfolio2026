The number on a digital marketing quote is not the cost of digital marketing, and that is the whole trick. Most of the money goes somewhere the quote does not show: the media spend, your own cost of serving the customer, and the gaps the scope leaves open. This guide breaks the price into its three real layers, the common pricing models, and how to compare two quotes without being fooled by the sticker.

Exact fees and platform costs change, and they differ between providers, so confirm current numbers with each firm. This is a pricing framework, not a quote from anyone.

## The three layers of the real cost

When someone says "digital marketing costs X," they usually mean only the first layer. The full picture is three:

| Layer | What it is | Where people go wrong |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Media / ad spend | The money sent to the platforms for reach | Underestimating it, or overestimating it without a goal |
| 2. Service / agency fee | The cost of the work, strategy and running | Reading only this, and forgetting layers 1 and 3 |
| 3. Your cost to serve | The cost of the customer the marketing brings | Forgetting that reach without capacity is wasted spend |

A realistic budget holds all three. A plan that budgets the fee but not the media or the serving cost will look affordable and then fall over when the customer arrives and you cannot serve them.

## The common pricing models

You will meet a few shapes, and they are not the same thing wearing the same name.

- **A fixed monthly fee** for ongoing management: predictable, good for steady work, and you should know exactly what is included each month.
- **A percentage of ad spend**: aligns the fee with the media you are running, so the fee scales with the spend.
- **A project fee** for a setup or a build: one-time, tied to a deliverable, not to the ongoing work.
- **Performance elements**: sometimes added, and they should be tied to a customer-level number, not to a click.

The model matters more than the number, because the model tells you what is being bought and what is expected. Ask which model a quote uses and what is in and out, before you compare the figure.

## How to compare two different quotes

The mistake is comparing two numbers as if they are the same product. They are not, because the scope and the effort differ. Normalize them instead:

1. **Ask each for the cost per customer** they expect, tied to a real goal, not a click.
2. **List what is included** in each: strategy, the channels, the reporting, the owner.
3. **Add the media spend** each assumes, and your own cost to serve.
4. **Compare the totals**, not the fees.

A cheaper fee that excludes measurement or the owner is more expensive in the end, because you pay for a result you cannot see. The comparison that matters is cost per customer with the scope written down, which is also the method in [how to evaluate a digital marketing company in Nepal](/blog/best-digital-marketing-company-nepal/).

## What the money should be able to prove

Whichever model and quote you pick, the plan it buys should be able to answer one question: **what does one customer cost, and is that under what that customer is worth to you?** If the plan cannot produce that number, you are buying reach, not marketing, and you will not know if it is working. This is the reason to insist on a customer-level metric, covered in [what a digital marketing agency in Nepal does](/blog/digital-marketing-agency-nepal/).

## A budgeting habit that keeps the cost honest

Hold three numbers on one page and update them each period:

- **The media spend** you actually ran.
- **The service fee** you paid, against the scope.
- **The cost per customer** the two produced together.

When the cost per customer is below your customer's value and falling, you are spending well. When it is high or unknown, that is the number to fix, and it is more useful than any sticker price. **Bottom line:** the price of digital marketing is three layers, not one, and the quote shows only the middle. Budget all three, ask which pricing model you are buying, and compare quotes on the expected cost per customer with the scope in writing. Do that and you stop reading a sticker and start reading the real cost.
