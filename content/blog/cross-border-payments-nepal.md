Paying across a border from Nepal is not one problem; it is three tools doing three jobs, and the failure is always using the wrong tool for the job. This guide lines up the tools, the jobs, and the limit that sits underneath all of it.

Fees, supported corridors and NRB rules change, so confirm the current ones. This is general guidance, not financial advice.

## The three tools, by job

| Job | The tool that fits | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Pay a foreign vendor once | An international card | That online/international is on, and the limit clears it |
| Pay a foreign vendor repeatedly | A receiving/transfer platform the vendor accepts | That the vendor actually accepts it, and the all-in cost |
| Move a known sum abroad | A bank wire or transfer service | The limit, the document, the spread |

The card is the easy one. The platform is the recurring one. The wire is the documented one. Pick by the job, and the "which is best" question disappears.

## The local wallets are not in this list

This is the mistake that costs the most time. eSewa and Khalti are for inside Nepal. They do not pay a US or EU vendor, and they are not a cross-border tool. If your plan is "I will use my local wallet for my foreign bill," that plan will not run; the money has to move through a card or a cross-border service instead. The full foreign-payment picture is in [how to pay for foreign hosting from Nepal](/blog/pay-foreign-hosting-from-nepal/) and [PayPal alternatives in Nepal](/blog/paypal-alternatives-nepal/).

## The limit underneath all of it

Every one of the three is moving foreign currency, which is a regulated flow in Nepal. The habit that keeps any of them clean is the same: confirm the current ceiling and the document it needs at your branch before the payment, and keep the record of the transaction. See [NRB dollar card rules and limits](/blog/nrb-dollar-card-rules-nepal/) for the shape of that.

## A sensible default

For the one-off, use the card. For the recurring, find the platform the vendor accepts and price its spread. For a known sum, run the wire and keep the document. Keep the local wallets out of all three, and confirm the limit before the big ones.

**Bottom line:** cross-border payment from Nepal is a card for the one-off, a vendor-accepted platform for the recurring, and a documented wire for the known sum, with the NRB limit checked underneath. Match the tool to the job and the local wallets stay where they belong, at home.
