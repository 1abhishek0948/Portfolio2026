An e-commerce site in Nepal is not the same as an e-commerce site anywhere else, because three local things change the whole build: the payment gateways your customers actually use, the delivery reality, and how people in your market expect to pay. Get those three right and the platform choice becomes almost trivial. Get them wrong and no amount of UI polish saves the store. This guide walks the three, and how to think about the cost of the build around them.

Gateway coverage, fees and delivery prices change, so confirm the current positions with each provider. This is practical guidance, not a recommendation of any service.

## The three local things that decide the build

| Local factor | What it changes | The question to answer first |
|---|---|---|
| Payments | Which gateways the site must integrate | How will my customers actually pay? |
| Delivery | How the checkout and tracking must work | What is the real last-mile cost and time? |
| Customer expectations | What the storefront must support | What do they expect to do before they pay? |

Most store failures in a new market are not code failures; they are a mismatch between the store and how the market buys. Answer the three questions with evidence, and the rest is build.

## Choosing the platform: hosted vs custom

The platform question is usually "a hosted store or a custom one," and the honest answer is that it depends on how proven your flow is.

- **A hosted platform** gets you live fast, with updates and checkout handled. It is the right first step when you are still testing which products sell.
- **A custom store** earns its build cost when your local payment and delivery flow is the bottleneck, or when you need behavior a platform will not give you.

A common, sensible path is to **start on a platform to prove demand, then move to a custom build once the flow is proven and the local pain is real.** Building custom on day one is a lot of cost against a guess.

## The payment layer (the one that actually matters here)

In Nepal, a store that only accepts an international card will miss most of its market. The realistic mix is local digital wallets and payment services, plus cash on delivery, because a large share of customers still expect to pay on receipt. The practical questions:

- Which **local wallets and payment services** do my customers use?
- Does **cash on delivery** fit my product and my delivery region?
- What are the **gateway fees and settlement times**, and do they fit my margin?

The integration work is the technical part, but the decision is a market question. Get the answer first, then the build follows. For the setup specifics, see the [payment gateway setup guide](/blog/payment-gateway-setup/).

## The delivery layer (the cost people forget)

Delivery is where an online store in Nepal quietly dies. The website is one cost; the last mile is another, and it is usually bigger. Plan for:

- **Delivery cost and time** by region, not just "we deliver nationwide."
- **Returns**, which are a real operating cost, not an exception.
- **Cash-on-delivery reconciliation**, if you offer it, because some orders will not convert.

Budget delivery and returns separately from the website, and test the flow on a few real orders before you scale the catalog. A store that is cheap to build and expensive to deliver is not cheap.

## How to think about the cost, without a number

The build cost is a small slice of the total. A useful way to budget is to hold three numbers separate:

1. **The build**, the one-time cost of the site.
2. **The payments and delivery**, the per-order cost that scales with sales.
3. **The maintenance**, the ongoing cost of keeping it live and correct.

Most businesses under-budget number two and three and over-focus on number one. A store that is cheap to build but has a terrible per-order cost is expensive to run. Know all three before you commit.

## A launch order that fits the market

1. **Answer the three questions** with real evidence, not guesses.
2. **Start on a hosted platform** and prove that a product sells.
3. **Add the local payment and delivery flow** that your customers actually use.
4. **Measure the per-order cost** and the returns rate on real orders.
5. **Only then decide** whether a custom build is worth the cost.

That order keeps you from paying custom-build money for an unproven flow, which is the most expensive mistake in the whole process.

**Bottom line:** an e-commerce site in Nepal is won or lost on payments, delivery, and customer expectations, not on the code. Answer those three with evidence, start on a platform to prove demand, and only move to custom when the local flow is genuinely the bottleneck. Budget the per-order and maintenance costs as real, and the store will survive its own launch.
